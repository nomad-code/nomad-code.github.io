El arte del centro

What Oaxaca's two museums reveal about who gets to present Indigenous culture. Source: nolanpatrick.substack.com

In Oaxaca, art is everywhere — talleres for weaving and engraving, murals, street artisans — and so is the graffiti that answers back. One line, “El arte del centro no me representa,” separates the art made for tourists and the state from the art of the people. Patrick reads two museums against that line: one that presents pre-Hispanic objects as timeless “fenómenos artísticos” stripped of place and creator, and one that historicizes craft down to the clay. The gap between them names a whole way of seeing.

“‘El arte del centro’ is not a specific work or artist or style — it is any art that is top-down, that is centralized, that owes its presentation to a center.”

01

¿Dónde está el centro?

The same objects — Zapotec, Mixtec, from Monte Albán and Santa María Atzompa — can be arranged two ways. Pull them into a center and they lose their names: “presented like artistic phenomena,” “without time, place, or creator.” Or let them stay in their pueblos, and the center of gravity moves to the living communities themselves. 02

Dos museos, un mismo objeto

Patrick sets two exhibitions side by side. The Museo de Arte Prehispánico de México holds painter Rufino Tamayo's archaeological collection; its introductory text, by curator Fernando Gamboa, frees the objects from the anthropologist's gaze by presenting them as pure form. The Museo de las Culturas de Oaxaca, inside the Templo de Santo Domingo, does the opposite — it “delights in specificity and process.”

03

Cinco salas, dos colores

In the Prehispánico museum the objects are arranged across five galleries “by region and epoch,” each with color-coded vitrine backdrops. But the color does the sorting the culture should: what a visitor carries out is an impression of “pink and purple rooms,” not the difference between Zapotec and Mixtec. Hover a room.

The critique made literal: vitrine color is arbitrary; it carries no cultural information, yet it becomes the exhibit's organizing memory. The lime label below is the specificity the display withholds — a named people, a place, a date.
04

La tradición se renueva

The Oaxaca museum's answer to the frozen object is a loop, not a timeline. Citing Zubiri, its text calls Atzompa's potters “the same people but not ‘the same’” — each generation inherits a craft and remakes it. And the craft is bound to matter: when Guadalajara grew, the clay deposits changed, and the potters adapted. Hover the loop.

Specificity as method: where the center presents a timeless object, the Oaxaca museum presents a practice that changes — down to the clay. Tradition here is a loop that each generation re-draws, not a relic behind glass.
05

El doble vínculo del indigenismo

Back on the street, Patrick reaches for scholar A.S. Dillingham's idea of the “double bind of indigenismo.” The Mexican state, Dillingham argues, “rhetorically celebrated Indigenous people, but it also cast them as a problem to be overcome.” One policy, two contradictory pulls — and the museum of the center performs both at once. Hover the forks.

Both branches, one origin: Dillingham's bind is that celebration and erasure are the same gesture. Read through the essay, the “artistic phenomena” of the center's museum is the celebratory branch — and it erases living communities all the same.

07

El arte del centro

The essay ends not with a villain but a definition. “El arte del centro” is a relation, not an object: whenever art is presented top-down, from a center, on a people's behalf, that is the art of the center — whether it hangs in Mexico City or is sold to a tourist in the Zócalo. The Oaxaca museum shows the alternative isn't erasure but specificity.

“‘El arte del centro’ is not a specific work or artist or style — it is any art that is top-down, that is centralized, that owes its presentation to a center.”
