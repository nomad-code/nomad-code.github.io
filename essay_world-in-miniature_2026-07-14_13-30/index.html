The world in miniature

The Princeton University Art Museum's encyclopedic collection — the essay's argument, drawn. Source: nolanpatrick.substack.com

Reopened in October 2025 in a building nearly twice its former size, the Princeton University Art Museum holds more than 117,000 works spanning the globe and five thousand years. Patrick reads it as a specific kind of institution — the “universal survey museum” of Duncan and Wallach — but a new variation on it. Where the classic version monuments state power and the American one (the Met) monuments private fortune, Princeton's runs on the university: networks of academic knowledge and alumni wealth, bringing the world, in miniature, to campus.

“Its layout aims to bring the world to campus” — yet the museum, the essay argues, still remains a demonstration of power.

01

Three ways to monument power

The universal survey museum, in Duncan and Wallach's account, casts every visitor in “the role of an ideal citizen” — heir to a civilized past — through a “programmed experience.” Historically it was a tool of state power. The American variation, the Met, was built by private industrialists as “a monument that would equate their interests with those of the public.” Patrick's claim: Princeton is a third variation — the university museum, powered by knowledge and alumni fortune. The classic model. The museum monumentalizes the state; the visitor is positioned as an “ideal citizen,” heir to a civilized past.

The American model (the Met). Private wealth builds it — “a monument that would equate their interests with those of the public.”

Princeton's model. Power flows from the university — academic knowledge production and the fortune of its alumni.

The constant is the shell — the source of power is what changes. Princeton's director, James Steward, calls the reopened museum “a hub and gathering space”; its layout, the essay notes, “aims to bring the world to campus.” But bringing the world in, Patrick argues, is still a way of demonstrating who holds it.

02

From a march to a lattice

The old universal museum arranged cultures as a progression — a ranked climb toward Western modernity, the West as the summit. Princeton's second floor is instead structured as nine pavilions “that vary in levels of spatial enclosure,” a plan that flattens the collection across time and space and sets cultures in dialogue rather than in rank. Same encyclopedic ambition; a different geometry of power.

Read as geometry, not metric: the essay describes the arrangement — a progression versus a flattened set of nine pavilions — not a measured quantity. The reframe is spatial: the plan refuses the ranked march the universal museum was invented to stage.
03

The cabinet of curiosities

The museum's Asian-art display is titled “World in Miniature.” It revives the Wunderkammer — the Qing-dynasty and Euro-American cabinets that gathered varied objects together “to delight the eye and to bring the world, in miniature, into the home or gallery,” prizing curiosity over typological order. The Princeton Collects show extends the metaphor: ≈150 alumni-donated works, called “a celebration of the Museum in miniature.” Hover a compartment.

The oldest way to hold the world: Tony Bennett and Krzysztof Pomian trace the museum's curiosity back to cabinets fixed on “all that was most rare and most inaccessible.” Princeton stages it as pleasure — “encounters that excite the imagination” — and, in Princeton Collects, as a portrait of its own donors.
04

Power through networks

If the classic museum's power was the state and the Met's was fortune, Princeton's is two networks feeding one hub. On one side, knowledge production: wall labels written by named academics and practitioners, many not Princeton-affiliated — an NYU PhD candidate, the museum's Chief Conservator, an Alaska Southeast professor, a Minneapolis theater director, a printmaker, a Japanese tea expert. On the other, alumni fortune: more than 200 benefactors, some 2,000 works given, and about two-thirds of the new building's cost.

The museum as crossroads: designed with multiple entrances, it convenes knowledge and wealth into one collection. The essay's point is that this convening is the demonstration of power — just rendered in networks rather than in a crown.

06

A demonstration of power

The essay's conclusion doesn't fault the museum for its ambition — it names the ambition's source. The universal museum was always a way of showing who holds the world. Princeton shows it differently: through participation in knowledge production and dependence on the university's global networks. The world in miniature is also a map of the power that assembled it.

The Princeton University Art Museum remains a demonstration of power — differing from other universal museums in the extent to which it emphasizes participation in knowledge production, and in its dependence on the university's global networks.
