The repointed axis

Doris Duke's presence at the Shangri La Museum of Islamic Art — the essay's argument, drawn. Source: nolanpatrick.substack.com

In 1935 a 22-year-old tobacco heiress honeymooned east across “the Orient” and came home with a house. Shangri La — named for the utopia in Hilton's Lost Horizon, built on Black Point, Honolulu, 1935–37 — now holds one of the largest collections of Islamic art in the United States. Patrick's essay argues the museum never resolved a founding tension: it calls itself the country's only museum dedicated exclusively to Islamic art, yet its rooms, labels, and sightlines keep asking you to see that art as Doris Duke saw it.

“Islamic art regularly competes with Doris Duke herself for the museum's attention.”

01

One niche, two axes

The collection's most prominent object is a 13th-century mihrab, made for the shrine of a tomb in Veramin, Iran. A mihrab has one job: to orient worshipers along an axis toward Mecca. Installed in Duke's Living Room, it was given a new job — anchoring a visual axis that runs the other way, out through the house to Le'ahi (Diamond Head) and the Pacific, “via Doris Duke's collecting interests.” Toggle the eras; watch the same object serve two different masters.

Sacred axis. The niche points the room — and everyone praying in it — toward Mecca.

Scenic axis. The niche anchors a sightline to the Hawaiian landscape; artworks line the route.

The repurposing, per the museum's own label: the Living Room “frames a visual axis that connects the landscapes of Hawai‘i to the mihrab… Along this axis a multitude of artworks showcase the variety of interests that guided Duke's collecting practice.” Patrick's objection is structural — the museum details the mihrab's origins, but “fails to explain how Duke's vision can be justified as the best way to present the mihrab to a contemporary audience.”
02

Whose house, when

The house spent far longer as Duke's private sanctuary than it has spent as anyone's museum — 56 years private against 24 public. She guarded it from “the prying press” (Proctor-Tiffany), then in 1965 quietly amended her will to hand it over. Hover the dots.

03

The map speaks two languages

The museum's introductory labels define Islamic art expansively — “a diverse, vibrant and ever-evolving set of artistic styles.” But walk the floor plan and count the names: three spaces carry the vocabulary of Islamic art history; five still answer to Duke's domestic nomenclature. Patrick: “despite introducing Islamic art as an organizing element, Duke and her collecting practice ultimately dominate the museum, prompting visitors to adopt her vision.” Hover a room for its label.

04

The honeymoon as pilgrimage

Saïd, quoting Schwab, describes nineteenth-century Orientalism as “an amateur or professional enthusiasm for everything Asiatic, which was wonderfully synonymous with the exotic, the mysterious, the profound, the seminal” — and observes that for a century, “the Orient was a place of pilgrimage.” Patrick places Duke's 1935 honeymoon squarely in that lineage: a journey “defined by escapism rather than religiosity” that extended the Orient from North Africa to Hawai‘i, “progressing as far east as possible before a return to the U.S. mainland.” Hover the stops.

Saïd's constraint: “fields of learning, as much as the works of even the most eccentric artist, are constrained and acted upon by society, by cultural traditions, by worldly circumstance…” Even a progressive, independent collector — and the essay grants Duke was one — inherits the pilgrimage's frame.
05

Where the visitors' attention goes

The framing works — on the audience. Setthamalinee's 2006 visitor survey found people came away more interested in learning about Doris Duke than about Islamic culture; the draw was “the exoticism of celebrity and the ultra-rich,” not “the Orient” itself. The museum leans in, staging visitors “as though they are in attendance at a glamourous Duke gathering.”

Read honestly: the survey reported a direction of interest, not a percentage split — branch weights here are qualitative, not measured shares. The finding stands either way: at a museum “dedicated exclusively to Islamic art,” the founder out-draws the art.
06

The scholars' chorus

Patrick builds the argument from six voices — a framework, a fair hearing for Duke, the evidence against the framing, and a way out.

07

The offered distance

The essay's fix isn't demolition — it's a third position. Keep the house, keep Duke in the story, but move the visitor off her axis: a vantage from which both orientations, the sacred one and the scenic one, are visible as history.

Both orientations in one field of view — the qibla the mihrab was built for, and the sightline Duke gave it. Neither erased; neither adopted.

“…rather than positioning its audience within Duke's perspective, it can offer them distance from it, enabling visitors to develop a more critical understanding of the site and its place in the history of Western thought about the East.”
