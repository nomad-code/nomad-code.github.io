9 bar was never ideal.

The espresso puck is not a rigid filter — it's a spring. Squeeze it harder and it squeezes back, closing its own pores. The Warsaw poroelasticity paper finally put data under what profiling nerds suspected for a decade: flow through the puck peaks somewhere around 3–6 bar, and 9 sits on the wrong side of the apex. The sacred number was a hardware accident that got reverse-engineered into gospel.

The physics

Darcy lies about pucks

Darcy's law is the textbook story: flow scales with pressure. It assumes the medium doesn't move. Coffee moves.

Illustrative — the paper's measured curves are per-coffee · more pressure ≠ more flow

The dogma leans on Darcy's law — flow through a porous bed is proportional to the pressure across it:

Q = k·A·ΔP / (μ·L)   with k fixed

That holds only while permeability k is a constant. A tamped puck isn't. Under load it compacts: porosity drops, channels close, and k becomes a function of the very pressure you're adding. The Warsaw team brewed hundreds of instrumented shots and showed the pressure–flow relationship is nonlinear with an apex — push past it and extra bars buy you the same flow, then less flow, plus more compaction and channeling risk.

Their clean result comes from the late-shot regime (~30–40 s in, solubles gone) where the puck is a pure poroelastic material. The paper is deliberately phenomenological — it skips the messy first seconds to nail the mechanism.

Lance's contribution is the replication: as he puts it, this is the first time it's been shown with actual experience, with actual data, that the flow does change — and where exactly it's changing. Not forum vibes. Instrumented shots, on his own machines.

The rant proper

Where 9 actually came from

Nobody derived 9 bar. Hardware shipped it, then the industry worked backwards to call it optimal.

1948

Gaggia's spring lever. The first crema-era machines were springs: pressure peaked around 8–10 bar and declined through the shot. Nobody chose the number — the spring did.

1961

Faema E61. The motorized pump replaced the spring and needed a setpoint. It got one in the lever's neighborhood: 9. A mechanical accident became a spec.

1990s–2010s

Canonization. Standards bodies, certification courses and spec sheets enshrined 9 bar as "ideal extraction pressure." Home-machine marketing sprinted past it to "15 bar" as if more were better — the exact opposite of what the puck does.

2020s

The cracks. Turbo shots, 6-bar flat profiles and lever revivals kept beating the dogma empirically. Blind tastings didn't defend 9. The physics explanation just hadn't landed yet.

2026

The receipt. Waszkiewicz et al. in Physics of Fluids: the puck self-regulates flow poroelastically; the apex lives around 3–6 bar. As Lance says — it doesn't seem like nine bar was ever what was ideal. It was just a byproduct.

Honest edges

What the paper doesn't say

The video is careful here, and the rant should be too. The apex is real; its address varies.

One coffee

n = 1 bean

The study used a single coffee. The apex will move with your setup — unimodal vs bimodal grinder, light vs dark roast, dose and basket. 3–6 bar is a zone, not a setpoint.

The transient

The first ~5 seconds still rule

The paper models the late, clean regime and deliberately skips the transient — yet the shot's fate is largely dictated in those first seconds, where the dynamics are far more complex. The apex result governs the body of the shot, not the whole story.

Bar practice

What changes at the machine

The takeaways that survive the caveats.

Rule one

Chase flow, not the gauge

The gauge measures what the pump pushes; the cup only cares what the puck passes. Watch flow rate (or use a machine that does) and treat pressure as the knob, not the goal.

Rule two

Choking? Don't add pressure

The counterintuitive one: a choked shot compacts further under extra bars. Backing off pressure — or grinding coarser — opens the puck. More force makes a stuck puck more stuck.

Rule three

6 flat beats 9 by default

A ~6 bar flat profile lands in or near the apex zone for most setups, extracts evenly, and lowers channeling risk. Gentle low-pressure preinfusion respects the transient that sets the shot.
